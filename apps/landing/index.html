Who depends on whom, and the sentence that proves it.

A dependency graph of public companies, built only from what those companies have already disclosed. Every edge carries the sentence in the filing that supports it. If we cannot show you the sentence, we do not make the claim.

The asymmetry this is built on

When a factory burns down, everyone can name the companies hit directly. The companies hit next - because they quietly buy something from the ones that were hit - are much harder to find, and nobody publishes a list of them.

You cannot get that list from the obvious direction. A large buyer has no reason to publish its suppliers and every reason not to. But a small supplier is required to disclose a customer worth more than about 10% of its revenue, because losing that customer would take a tenth of the business with it. So the small company names the large one, and we read it from that end.

“Sales to General Motors Company (GM) were approximately 44 % of our consolidated net sales in 2025, 42 % in 2024, and 39 % in 2023.”

Dauch Corporation · Form 10-K · filed 2026-02-13 · SEC EDGAR

General Motors does not publish that. Dauch has to.

How good it is, measured rather than claimed

We read the cited sentences by hand and counted. Two tiers of evidence, measured separately, because averaging them would hide the difference that matters.

EvidenceJudgedCorrectMethod
disclosed % the filing states a percentage 2828 · 100%exhaustive, not sampled
named only the filing names a counterparty 6041 · 68.3%seeded random sample

Every percentage we display is the right number for the right company (21 of 21). Where a sentence does not let us attribute a figure safely, we show no figure rather than guess - that applies to 7 of the 28.

The first audit measured 62.5%. Getting here meant removing edges: 803 down to 207. The sample, the seed and every judgement are in the repository.

What we also found, and would rather say out loud

We measured recall by sweeping the whole corpus for disclosure-shaped sentences and reading 60 of them. Of those 60:

  • 48 name no company at all. “One customer accounted for 42.86% of total sales.” That satisfies the regulator completely and tells us nothing.
  • 7 name a party outside the markets we cover - a government department, companies listed in Taiwan or China, a university.
  • 5 name a public company we cover, and we captured all 5.

So roughly one disclosure in twelve names a counterparty we can resolve. That is a ceiling on the method, not a defect in the code, and it is the honest answer to why the graph is small.

Rules we hold ourselves to

  • Every edge cites a sentence. chunk_id is NOT NULL with a foreign key - a constraint the data cannot violate, not a convention we remember.
  • The model never scores anything. It reads one sentence and returns structure. Scoring is deterministic and versioned, so the same inputs give the same number forever.
  • No backtest can see the future. Every read goes through one function that filters by publication date. Replay a 2021 event and the graph correctly returns nothing, and says why.
  • No recommendations. We show who is exposed and the evidence. We never tell anyone what to buy or sell.
  • Public sources only. Regulatory filings, fetched by our own code. No employer systems, no licensed feeds, no credentials.

The live app

Open the globe →

This one has the data in it. Pick a company, read the sentence its dependency was extracted from, and follow the link through to that filing on the SEC's own servers. All 363 cited edges are there, all 571 claims, all 26 events, and every one of the 14,738 filing passages behind them.

What it is not. A deployed page cannot reach a database on your computer - a browser refuses an HTTPS page's request to http://localhost - so this copy is served from a frozen snapshot of the API's own responses rather than from a live query. Two things follow, and both are worth knowing before you judge what you are looking at. It holds 586 companies, not the 23,020 in our coverage universe: the universe came from vendor market files that our own source register marks not cleared for publication, so what we publish is the subset that actually carries evidence - a company we cited, read a filing of, or reached by propagation. And it is a snapshot, so it answers the questions the interface asks and not arbitrary ones.

Run it locally and both limits go: make up, then open localhost:5173, and the same interface queries the real database over the full universe.

Run it

make dev        # Postgres + PostGIS, migrations, reference data
make pipeline   # filings -> chunks -> cited claims -> graph -> exposure
make api        # read API on :8000
make web        # globe on :5173

make eval       # what is in the graph right now
make audit      # every concentration-backed relationship, with its sentence
make backtest   # replay 26 real events, fail on regression
make test       # 60 tests
make alerts     # ingest stopped / precision dropped / read path over budget
