01

What we build

An exposure graph of public companies, built from what those companies disclose about themselves, rendered on a globe, with world events propagating through it live.

A listed company's dependencies are already public. They are buried in risk factors, segment notes, supplier concentration disclosures and analyst Q&A — in prose, across thousands of documents, in language that changes every quarter. No one reads all of it, so nobody holds the whole graph.

We read all of it. For each company we extract the entities it depends on — suppliers, customers, plants, ports, shipping routes, commodities, jurisdictions — and the strength and direction of each dependency. That becomes a graph. Then real world events enter and travel through it.

The output we owe the user

“A Hormuz closure touches 34 companies in your coverage. Ranked by disclosed dependence. Here is the sentence in each filing that says so, and the earnings call where management was asked about it.”

Named entity, quantified exposure, and a citation to a primary document for every single claim. If we cannot cite it, we do not assert it.

Why citation-first is the whole architecture

Every product in this space produces confident numbers with no provenance, which is exactly why analysts do not trust them. Our differentiator is the opposite discipline: each edge in the graph carries the document, the page, and the exact sentence it came from. That single constraint gives us three things at once — a defence against hallucination, an audit trail an institutional buyer will insist on, and a viva answer that holds up under any amount of pushing.

Two buyers, one graph

WhoQuestion they bringWhat the graph returns
Corporate procurement & strategy “Which of my suppliers is exposed to this, and who are their alternatives?” Second- and third-tier dependency chains their own vendors disclosed
Buy-side and sell-side analysts “Which names in my coverage does this event actually reach?” Ranked exposure with filing citations they can paste into a note

The three surfaces

  • Live view — the globe with the exposure graph drawn on it, current events plotted, affected entities ranked in a side panel.
  • Replay — scrub back through any historical window and watch a real past disruption propagate, then check it against what those companies actually reported the following quarter.
  • Simulation — define a shock, run it forward, get a ranked impact table with citations and a written brief.

The line we do not cross

We ship exposure data and cited research, never a recommendation. No BUY or SELL, no target prices, no portfolio advice. That is a real and well-understood distinction: data and factual research are unregulated, recommendations are not. It costs us nothing — our buyers want the graph, not our opinion — and it keeps the project clear of financial regulation entirely.

02

Data foundation

The advantage here is knowing exactly which sources matter and how they behave. That knowledge is portable. The data itself has to be rebuilt clean.

Read this before the first commit

Anything belonging to an employer — production databases, servers, credentials, licensed vendor feeds, internal code — stays out of this project unless you have written permission. Most employment agreements assign work product to the employer, and licensed financial data almost always forbids redistribution regardless of who is asking. A project built on that foundation cannot be published, cannot be demoed publicly, and cannot be sold.

The good news is that none of it is necessary. Everything below is public or free, and knowing which sources to use and how they misbehave is the part that actually took years to learn — and that part is yours to keep.

Carry across
  • Which sources exist and what each is good for
  • Pipeline architecture patterns
  • Hard-won edge cases — fiscal period quirks, half-year reporting, transcript-to-filing matching
  • Your own skills and judgement
Leave behind
  • Any employer database contents or exports
  • Credentials, certificates, server access
  • Licensed vendor data of any kind
  • Code written on company time

What we build on instead

SourceGives usTerms
SEC EDGAR full-text + XBRL10-K, 10-Q, 8-K for every US filer — risk factors, segments, concentrationsPublic domain. Free API, fair-use rate limits
Exchange announcement feedsASX, NSE/BSE, LSE regulatory disclosuresPublic regulatory filings
Company IR sitesEarnings call transcripts and decks, published by the companies themselvesPublic. Check each site's terms

Together these cover several thousand listed companies across the US, India and Australia with no licence fee and no redistribution restriction. That is more than enough graph for twenty weeks — and every byte of it is ours to publish, demo and sell.

Scope the universe deliberately

Do not try to cover every listed company on earth. Pick one sector, one market, one filing year for phase 1 — something with genuinely global supply chains, like semiconductors, pharmaceuticals or auto components. Depth on 200 companies beats a thin layer on 8,000, and it makes the accuracy measurement tractable.

Why the split

Disclosure ingestion is slow, bursty and seasonal — everything arrives in the weeks after quarter end. Event ingestion is continuous. Extraction is expensive and runs on a backlog. The API must answer in under 200ms and must never be blocked by any of them. Four services means each scales, fails and deploys independently; they share one database and one models package, so the cost of the split is close to zero.

Read path versus write path

PathTriggerBudget
Ingest & extractNew filing or transcript appearsHours. Backlog is fine, it runs behind
Event & propagateRelevant event landsMinutes. Recompute exposures, fire alerts
ReadUser opens a view or scrubs the timeline<200ms. Everything precomputed and cached

Never compute exposure on a page load. The user reads precomputed rows; the workers do the thinking. This is what keeps the globe feeling instant.

04

Extraction pipeline

Turning prose into graph edges. This is the hardest part of the project and the part worth the most — budget accordingly.

01
Fetch and store rawPull the document, store the original bytes before parsing anything. A parser bug must always be replayable without re-hitting the source. adapters/edgar.py
02
SegmentSplit into labelled sections — Item 1A Risk Factors, Item 7 MD&A, segment notes, Q&A versus prepared remarks. Section label is a strong prior on what kind of claim lives there, so never discard it.
03
Chunk with offsetsChunk for retrieval, but keep byte offsets back into the source document. Without offsets you cannot cite, and without citation the product has no defensible claim.
04
Embed and indexVectors into Qdrant, keyed by chunk id. This powers retrieval for extraction and the Q&A surface later.
05
Extract dependency claimsAn LLM reads targeted chunks and emits structured claims, never prose: subject company, relation type, object entity, geography, strength language, and the exact quoted span. A schema-validated tool call, so malformed output fails loudly rather than silently corrupting the graph.
06
Resolve entities“TSMC”, “Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company Ltd.” and “our Taiwanese foundry partner” are one node. Blocking plus fuzzy match plus an LLM tiebreak, with every merge decision logged and reversible.
07
Score and persist edgesConfidence from source section, language strength, corroboration across filings, and recency. Edges below threshold are stored but flagged, never silently dropped — a weak edge is still evidence.
08
Human review queueSample every extraction run into a review UI. Accept, reject, correct. This is both your quality gate and your labelled training set for a cheaper model later.

The extraction contract

class DependencyClaim(BaseModel):
    subject_company_id: int
    relation: Literal["supplies_to", "sources_from",
                      "operates_in", "ships_via",
                      "exposed_to_commodity"]
    object_entity: str
    object_type: Literal["company","country","port",
                         "route","commodity","facility"]
    strength: Literal["critical","significant","mentioned"]

    # citation — no claim is stored without these
    document_id: str
    section: str
    quote: str
    char_start: int
    char_end: int
The rule that protects the whole project

The LLM extracts and explains. It never scores. It reads a sentence and reports what that sentence says, with the span. Confidence, exposure weight and impact are computed afterwards by deterministic, version-controlled, unit-tested code. That makes our output reproducible, testable, and unchanged when a model version rolls forward — and it means “how does it decide” has an answer you can walk through.

Two constraints from the first migration

1. dependency_edge.chunk_id is NOT NULL. Enforce citation in the schema, not in a code review — schemas do not forget.

2. Row-level security on every tenant table, with the isolation test written in the same PR. One customer seeing another's watchlist is the single failure this project cannot recover from.

The query that has to be fast

-- which entities does this event physically reach?
SELECT e.id, e.type
FROM entity e
WHERE ST_DWithin(e.geom::geography, $1::geography, $2);

CREATE INDEX ON entity USING GIST (geom);
CREATE INDEX ON dependency_edge (object_id, relation);
06

Propagation engine

A bounded weighted walk from an event to the companies it reaches. Deterministic, tested, and explainable edge by edge.

01
Match geographyWhich entities fall inside the event's blast radius? Radius is a function of category — a plant fire is local, a strait closure reaches everything routed through it.
02
Seed the frontierThose directly-hit entities become hop 0, each with an initial weight from event severity and the entity's own exposure.
03
Walk the dependency edgesTraverse inbound edges, multiplying weight by edge strength and confidence with a decay per hop. Cap at three hops. Beyond that the signal is noise and the explanation stops being credible.
04
Aggregate per companyA company reached by several paths accumulates, sub-linearly — three weak paths are not one strong one. Keep the top paths; they are the explanation.
05
Calibrate to unitsMap the score onto a delay or disruption distribution fitted from historical episodes. Emit p50 and p90. Never a single false-precision number.
06
Explain with citationsOnly now does an LLM write prose — narrating the stored path, quoting the filing sentence behind every hop it mentions.

Calibration and backtesting

The engine is only credible if we measure it, and here we have an unusually clean way to do that: companies tell you afterwards what actually happened. Take a historical disruption, run the engine against the graph as it stood before, then check the next quarter's filings and calls for who actually reported an impact. That is a real labelled test set, generated by the same pipeline that builds the product.

From phase 3 the backtest runs in CI on every change to scoring logic, and the precision and recall numbers go in the PR description. If a change makes the model worse, the number says so before it merges.

Conventions we hold to

  • Errors are typed and actionable — a machine-readable code plus a sentence a user could act on. Never a bare 500 saying "an error occurred".
  • Every list endpoint paginates from the first commit.
  • Timestamps are UTC ISO-8601 everywhere; timezone display is the frontend's job.
  • Money is integer minor units with an explicit currency field. Never a float.
  • Any response containing a claim contains its citation. Enforced in the response model, not by convention.

Why Postgres and not a graph database

A dedicated graph database is the obvious-looking choice and the wrong one here. Our walk is bounded at three hops, our geography lives in PostGIS, and everything else is relational. One database means one backup story, one migration tool, one set of credentials and one thing to learn. Revisit only if a recursive CTE actually becomes the bottleneck — measure first.

Secrets — set this up on day one

Workflow

Pick these and stop debating. The debate costs more than the difference.

GitHub

Create an organisation, not a repo on one person's account — it outlives the project and nobody's grade depends on one login. One private repo. Then in Settings → Branches, add a rule on main:

  • Require a pull request before merging
  • Require one approving review — one, not two; five students need velocity
  • Require status checks to pass, including the secret scan
  • Block force pushes and deletions

Use GitHub Projects for the board — it links issues to PRs automatically, so it stays honest without anyone maintaining it.

Branch and PR flow

main                          protected · always deployable
├── feat/citation-panel       one branch per issue
├── fix/entity-merge-loop
└── chore/ci-qdrant-service

commit style   feat: · fix: · chore: · docs: · refactor: · test:
merge style    squash — one commit per issue on main
issue link     "Closes #47" in the PR body

Open the PR as a draft on day one of the branch, not when you have finished — reviewers see direction early, and it kills the week-long surprise PR nobody can meaningfully review. Keep PRs under roughly 400 changed lines; beyond that review quality collapses and people rubber-stamp.

CI pipeline

Runs on every PR. Cheapest checks first, fails fast:

secrets     gitleaks                           ~10s
lint        ruff · eslint                      ~20s
typecheck   mypy · tsc --noEmit                ~40s
unit        pytest · vitest                    ~1m
integration pytest + postgres/postgis/qdrant   ~2m
extraction  golden-set precision/recall        ~2m
e2e         playwright, one critical path      ~2m
preview     deploy, comment URL on the PR      ~2m

The extraction check is the unusual one and the most important: a fixed set of hand-labelled documents with known correct claims. Any change to prompts, chunking or resolution reports its precision and recall in the PR. Prompt changes without a measured effect are how extraction quality quietly rots.

Five rules that prevent group-project failure

  • Nobody works alone on a critical path for more than three days. Rotate pairing deliberately.
  • Integrate weekly, not at the end. The demo enforces it; nothing else will.
  • Write the interface first — agree the contract in packages/shared before either side builds against an imagined shape.
  • Two people must be able to deploy. Have the second person do a real deploy in week two to prove the doc works.
  • Every bug gets a failing test before the fix. This is how a codebase stays trustworthy with five people committing.

Design direction

Our own visual language, decided in week one and held to. The goal is a screen that looks like an instrument, not a template.

Banned in this repo

  • dark navy + purple gradient
  • glassmorphism cards
  • 1px white border at 10%
  • Inter / Space Grotesk
  • everything faintly glowing
  • rounded-2xl on every corner
  • four stat cards in a row
  • “Powered by AI”
  • huge empty hero

If a PR contains a purple gradient on a dark navy card, reject it on sight.

Pick a direction that comes from somewhere

DirectionVocabularyWhy it fits
Nautical chart (recommended) Fine hairlines, depth soundings as tiny numerals, rhumb lines, magenta reserved for hazards Means something for a product about routes and chokepoints, and is genuinely uncommon on screen
Flight-operations panel Near-black, amber and green phosphor, monospace numerals, hard edges Reads as serious infrastructure; supports very high information density
Swiss editorial / scientific White, large type-scale contrast, strict grid, black rules, one accent Looks like a research institution published it — strong for a citation-first product

Decide as a team in week one and write it into docs/DESIGN.md. A consistent mediocre direction beats an inconsistent brilliant one.

Rules regardless of direction

  • Two type families with real hierarchy. One for interface, one for numerals — tabular figures so columns align.
  • One accent, used rarely. It means “look here now.” Three appearances per screen, maximum.
  • Density is a feature. Analysts want more on screen, not less. Airy reads as “no data behind it.”
  • Motion must show causality — an event travelling an edge. Never decoration.
  • Design the empty, loading and error states. The clearest tell between student work and product work.
  • The citation is a first-class UI object, not a tooltip. Quote, source, section, link — always one click away, everywhere a claim appears.
  • Figma before code. Nobody should be inventing the visual language at 2am inside a component.

The globe has to earn its place

It fits because exposure is geographic — routes, ports, plants, jurisdictions. But it answers a question rather than decorating the page: clicking anything goes somewhere useful, there is a 2D map for reading geography properly, a node-link view for reading the graph properly, and it never blocks the data. If the globe is still loading, the numbers are already on screen.
